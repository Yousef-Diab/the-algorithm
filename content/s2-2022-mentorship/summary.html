The one idea underneath everything

Price is delivered by an algorithm. There is no buying or selling pressure — that is "an excuse used for what the market does and doesn't do". Algorithmic theory rests on time and price, and the code leans heavily on the time element: a price level is useless until time is considered, and time is useless unless price is at a key level. That is why a rectangle on the chart is not supply and demand, and why this is not auction theory.

All the algorithm does (P3 L3)It seeks discount to premium and premium to discount. Within that it reaches for liquidity — buy stops above old highs, sell stops below old lows — and for imbalances: creating a fair value gap, or returning into one to rebalance it. And it does all of it on the basis of time, then price. ICT calls this a gross oversimplification, and says it is by and large all these things do.
The question behind every session (P1 L2)"What is price most likely to draw towards? Is there an imbalance higher, or a liquidity pool lower?" That single question sets the bias. Everything after it is detail.

Bias before precision. The entries taught here are deliberately forgiving; what you must be precise about is the directional bias and where price is reaching for. It requires very little chart graffiti: a line at the level you want breached, and a line at the level you want price to reach into.

Part 1 — Foundations & The 2022 Model

What this mentorship is, and isn't (L1)

The model is deliberately stripped down. The full arsenal from the private mentorship does create exceptional traders, but most students get bogged down in the content and it creates analysis paralysis — a kid in a candy store, unsure what to eat first. You are moved through three stages: yearner → learner → earner, and the last one is not on a schedule.

The promises, and your side of the deal
  • You will learn things that repeat — a lot. You will not be perfect; no teacher provides 100% accuracy and he does not trade perfectly either.
  • Setups repeat with common characteristics but are never carbon copies — you are learning to recognise a familiar setup, not match a template.
  • No trade signals. Levels are pointed at beforehand for study, not as an enticement to take a live trade.
  • Your side: an independent mindset, no codependency, no cutting corners, do the homework — and backtest, backtest, backtest.

The objective is income-based, not home runs: 25 handles a week out of a market like the E-mini S&P and you can do very well. A handle is four ticks — $50 on ES ($12.50 a tick), $20 on NQ, $5 on the micros. This is not a scalping course: the model looks for a whole intraday price leg.

The four elements of the setup (L2)

1
A run on liquidity — buy stops taken, if you're bearish
2
A break in market structure — a short-term swing low broken
3
The imbalance that displacement leaves behind — the fair value gap
4
Price trades back up into the gap — that's the short

Enter in the gap; stop above the candle above the gap, or above the swing high that was run; exit where the liquidity is. Any time a significant move lower is expected, always anticipate a stop hunt on buy stops first — the Judas swing — and the reverse before a rally.

Don't chaseOnce a low has been taken out you are chasing, and a market order there invites slippage. You have to learn to sell short while the candle is going up. It feels wrong, and retail can't grasp it because they've been told to wait for confirmation — which is precisely what makes them late.

The fair value gap, candle by candle (L6)

The bearish criteria
  • Candle 1 is the high; its low must be traded below on the immediately following candle.
  • Candle 3 must trade with an extended low below candle 2 — and must not trade back up to candle 1's low.
  • That leaves the gap: from candle 1's low down to candle 3's high, with candle 2 in the middle. Mirror it for bullish.

The paint roller. Roll a loaded roller down a wall and the first stretch goes on thick, then it leaves porous pockets. You fix it by rolling back up over the same place — which is exactly what price does. The optimal bearish gap forms after a run into buy-side liquidity; nothing before that matters except that price traded above an old high. The easiest entry is a limit just above candle 3's high, with the stop above candle 1 (or candle 2 if you can afford less range).

Displacement — the range the gap must sit in (L5, L6)

The elephant in the paddling poolFill a children's pool, drop an elephant in — the water is obviously displaced. That is what you want in price. The move away must be energetic and animated, preferably closing beyond the short-term level. A lethargic drift is not enough, and a little wick through the low that comes straight back is not convincing.
No gap, no tradeIf there is no fair value gap inside the displacement range, you don't have a trade. You wait, or you go to another market.

The market structure shift, and the one condition (L3)

The ruleBreaking a short-term high is significant only if the run down before it traded into sell stops — below an old low, a double bottom, something retail views as support. Bearish is the mirror: breaking a short-term low matters only because buy stops were taken above old highs first. No liquidity taken, no shift.

Shift is used deliberately rather than break: an intraday shift means there is likely an intraday draw in that direction — it does not imply prolonged, multi-day movement. Where relative equal highs sit above an older high, use the equal highs.

The order block, defined properly (L3)

What it actually isAn order block is a change in the state of delivery. It is not "every down-closed candle is a bullish order block and every up-closed candle is a bearish one" — that is not the case. The reference point is the opening price of the series, because the algorithm remembers that opening price.

Combine the two: the opening price of the order block, inside the fair value gap, as a limit order. A high-probability bearish block is the one that has the gap, has taken liquidity, and sits at a market structure shift.

Internal and external range liquidity (L3, L6)

Internal range liquidity is a short-term high or low sitting inside the price leg you are retracing back into, with stops behind it — or an imbalance in that same range. External range liquidity is the stops resting beyond the range's high and low. Partials at the internal, the balance at the external. Where two gaps are stacked, take the higher one but let price trade into the lower first; if price runs into the opposite imbalance before reaching your area, nix the trade.

Don't roll the stop earlySharp pullbacks are intended to knock out anyone trailing too tightly right before the real leg. Use the first partial to quench the urge to move your stop. Only once a significant intermediate-term low is taken out can you roll the stop down to it.

Premium & discount (L2)

Above 50%
Premium — an expensive market; where you want to be selling
Below 50%
Discount — a cheap market; where a sell is looking to go

Low hanging fruit: take the closest target — an old low, or an imbalance. Don't get fancy and aim for the lowest low on the chart, because the market can deny you that. Let it run further without you.

Framing the day (L5)

New York local time — no exceptionsSet your charting platform to New York local time. Wherever you are, calibrate from that. Use any other zone and your levels won't line up with anyone else's.
Morning
8:30 (news events) to noon — preferably positioned before 11:00
Lunch
Noon–1:00 pm — a no-trade period. Not even in demo.
Afternoon
From 1:30 at the earliest, where an algorithmic macro starts running

A swing high is three candles: a candle with a lower high to its left and a lower high to its right (a swing low is the mirror). Whether the candles close up or down has no bearing at all — swing points are simply where the stops are placed. In three drives, the third drive need not clear the old high: each swing high builds more buy stops above the previous one, and informed money is already short.

The only two afternoon patterns you need
  • After 1:30, wait for a swing low to be violated — buy those sell stops and expect the level above to be taken.
  • If no swing low gets traded below, look for a sudden displacement higher, then a fair value gap; when price trades back down into it, buy. Reversed for the sell side.
That's it — no fifteen gimmicky names. Contracts roll on H (March), M (June), U (September), Z (December); around the first or second day of the week prior to expiration, trade whichever month has the larger open interest.

Daily bias, and what to do without one (L7)

The bias questionYou look at the daily chart to answer one thing: where is price likely to draw to next? Higher to an old high, or lower to an old low? Until it reaches that draw and puts in a higher low, you stay with that bias.

No read is not no trading. When consolidation makes the bias unclear you drop to smaller intraday timeframes and simply look for liquidity pools — run old highs, run old lows, be far more nimble. A surgical strike: take your handles and go. And time before price — algorithms run on time and price, so refer to time first (what's the important time? 8:30), then go left and find the price.

The other indices are used like indicators, without being indicators — the Dow and S&P read alongside the Nasdaq. A crack in correlation is confirmation of an idea you had already established; by itself a divergence between the averages means nothing, and hunting divergences with no narrative behind them is how accounts were blown in the '90s.

Part 2 — Order Blocks, Power of Three & Structure

Power of Three (L2, L3)

Accumulation
The opening price sits near the low of the day or session
Manipulation
Price trades lower, making some important low — the fake move
Distribution
It rallies, makes the high, and closes near the high of the day
Take the close out of itYou do not need to predict the closing price. All you need to know is whether this daily range is more likely to expand higher or lower than the opening price. That's it — that is the key to bias.

Miss the manipulation low and you should try to get long close to the opening price — the one he likes is 8:30. Draw it out in time and run the checklist: did we go below it, into the imbalance, take out a short-term low, hit an order block, was it an optimal trade entry?

How an order block is actually found (L2, L5, L6)

The definitionConsecutive down-close candles right before a price surge that has an imbalance. The block is the series, not the last candle before the move — "that is not my order block, so stop calling it one." High probability = your narrative is bullish, you see displacement, you mark the down-close candles behind it, and you anticipate a return into them.
The three ingredientsA high-probability bullish order block is the down-close candle, plus the imbalance created as price moves away from it, plus the narrative that price is going higher to reach for buy-side liquidity. "Period. That's it." There is no engulfing-candle requirement.
Not every down close is an order blockThe rally exists to lull in traders who chase it. People who don't understand order blocks see a down-close candle after a run-up, buy it, watch it work, and conclude that every down close after a run-up is a bullish block. It is not — the real context is the imbalance.

In an established swing the candles themselves are the order flow: in a bearish swing, up-close candles are your resistance and price should stay below them; in a bullish swing, down-close candles are your support and price should hold above them. The one permitted violation is a dip when a short-term low sits in close proximity — that's just a run on sell stops before reaccumulating.

The opening range, and the 8:30 embargo (L3)

How to build itThe day opens and rallies — that rally is the Judas swing. Take the range from the opening price up to that high and project the same distance below the opening price. That is your opening range, and it is where the day's setups form. If price leaves it, you can't chase it, no matter how good it looks later.

8:30 is the crosshairs time because the news embargo lifts and a batch of events is ushered in at once. Reports may be a smokescreen or a catalyst; there is often a buildup or drop right before the report with the real move starting after. On the calendar, red is high impact, orange medium, yellow low. Strip the entry down 5 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 minute, take the first gap you come to and stop; if there isn't one even on the 1-minute, you don't have a trade.

Market structure — the halos (L4)

1 halo
Short-term high
2 halos
Intermediate-term high
3 halos
Long-term swing high

The halo system classifies the level of a high rather than just labelling higher highs and lower lows; ICT credits this way of deciphering market structure to Larry Williams. It is why he trusted a short near the session high: the nesting showed an intermediate-term high with lower swing highs either side. You do not need the high of the day — you need to understand why price should be trading away from the level you enter at.

The lesson he had never taught before: rebalance creates a key level (L5)

Write this one downEvery single time price rebalances an imbalance, the swing created at that moment is an intermediate-term high or low. A candle passing down leaves a gap; when price trades back up and rebalances it, that high is an ITH. When price drops back and fills an imbalance left by a candle up, that low is an ITL.

An ITH should be higher than the short-term highs either side of it. When it isn't, the market is very weak and the algorithm is tipping its hand — it is only rebalancing. And if, after a rebalance ITH, the next short-term high trades above it, your trade idea is probably flawed: don't force it, go to the sidelines and wait for structure to get back in sync.

The only question that mattersBefore he sits in front of a chart: is price likely to go up for buy-side liquidity, or up to rebalance an imbalance? Or down to sweep short-term lows for sell-side liquidity, or down to rebalance an old imbalance below market price? That's it — four parts, one question.

Everything smaller is subordinate to the parent: the daily is the parent of the structure, so minor lower-timeframe swings adhere to it. Frame it top-down — daily for the logic, hourly to frame the trade, 15-minute as the bellwether for the actual get in and get out, lower only if the risk on the 15 doesn't suit you.

Applying it to forex (L1)

The same institutional order flow, dropped onto a cross. Frame a cross by its components: pull up the futures of each currency on the daily — euro strong, yen weak → EUR/JPY goes higher. Then the killzones: New York 7:00–10:00 am, London 2:00–5:00 am, New York local time. Intraday, two questions frame it: does it take out a swing low, and does it then take out a swing high?

Pyramiding, gearing, and the days you don't trade (L2, L4, L6)

Build the base firstThe biggest position goes in the initial entry — 3, then 2, then 1 — so every later add has equity behind it and the position can weather a deeper retrace. Buying one, then two, then three is an inverted pyramid: balancing on its point, wobbly, no foundation.
After a big overnight runA big move on NASDAQ, Dow or ES roughly 2:00–5:00 am → avoid the New York morning session entirely. Wait until the other side of lunch — 1:00 pm — and anticipate the lunch lows or the morning session lows being taken out. Expect chop, not precision.

And knowing when enough is enough: "you don't want to push your edge — if you keep pushing it, you dull it." A wick through a gap does not invalidate it: look at the bodies, and in volatile conditions permit a greater level of imperfection, using the gap as the entry idea and the stop as the premise.

Part 3 — Sessions, Targeting & the Daily Narrative

The workflow, step by step (L5)

Upper left
The daily chart — always. This is where analysis starts.
Lower left
The hourly chart.
Right (largest)
The 15-minute — the bellwether.
  1. The daily chart and the bias. "Your bias isn't going to be perfect — I'm not always perfect with my bias either." A bias is an idea you work within: shorts when bearish, longs when bullish, and you filter out the other side unless proven clearly wrong.
  2. Frame the target on previous daily highs and lows. Mark the last three days' highs and lows — that is where the liquidity pools sit and where the HFT algorithms attack. "You're never going to run out of trades, ever."
  3. The hourly, then the 15-minute. The 15 is the chart he returns to all day: it is influential for managing a position, for stalking a setup, and — most of all — it helps him trust his intraday bias.
  4. Refine on the 5-minute, then 4, 3, 2, 1. The higher-timeframe imbalance is the parent; the smaller timeframes are only fine-tuning.
Not on your phoneYou can manage a position on a phone. Entering a brand-new position on one, no — the data is too compressed to see the lay of the land.

What makes an order block valid (L5, L6)

The correctionAn order block is a change in the state of delivery, and it has to have an imbalance. Without the imbalance, there is no order block. That is exactly why this is not supply and demand — supply and demand requires fresh, untouched zones; he will cut straight through candles and still trade the level.

The bookmark analogy: the algorithm places a bookmark where the candle closes. You're reading a chapter, life interrupts, you mark the page. Everything that happens while you're away is the move; when you come back you pick up the same storyline — it was dropping, it paused, it goes back to dropping.

The 9:30 open (L2)

The pattern in one lineAt 9:30, if there is a fair value gap with a swing low created beneath it, expect that low to be taken out to flush the early buyers — then buy the original gap once price sweeps the low and trades back up into it.

You could buy at the open, but you don't know how far it will whip below that low and you can be painfully wrong. On FOMC you can trade the morning, but you have to be done early.

Multiple setups inside one session (L3)

One Friday produced six: three shorts in the morning off successive gaps after displacement, then — once the sell-side objective was met — three longs in the afternoon into the PM liquidity pool. The engineering behind the turn: price took the relative equal lows into the order block, selling retail on "support broken", so when price traded back up into those same lows retail sold, and those sellers need a counterparty. That is engineering buy-side liquidity on the basis of sell-side flow.

The session rules
  • Morning 8:30–noon; lunch noon–1:00; PM session after it.
  • No entries during lunch — but you can take profits in it. A limit at the objective can fill while you're away.
  • No more than four trades a day — two in the morning, two in the afternoon.
  • Old highs are a premium array until price trades above them, after which the old high becomes a discount array — which is when a broken high can act as support. Once the liquidity target is met, stop using it.
Trades are busesSome days there won't be many retracements — one or two, or it moves at the open and that's it. You have no right to be angry about missing a trade. They come around on a schedule "like mass transit"; be happy your analysis spoke with that much clarity.

Swing projections and where to anchor a fib (L3)

Anchor to the highest body and the lowest body — the open or close, it makes no difference. "I'm showing you how the algorithm is going to read the price — the wicks and the tails are all distractions." The precision only pays when the rest of the narrative is incorporated: the order block it wants to reach, the relative equal lows where liquidity is known to be, and the measurement, all landing together. Use the wrong swing high and low and you get nothing — "that's why everybody that uses Fibonacci struggles."

The forex session (L4)

FX killzone
7:00–10:00 am New York — after 10:00, generally no new trades unless there's a news driver
Index futures
8:30–11:00 am; he'll still take a trade up to 10:40 or 10:45
News extension
A high-impact driver at 10:00 extends the window to 11:00/11:30; crude inventories around 10:30 do the same for CAD
Institutional price levels & the 8:30 ruleThe big figure and the 00, 20, 50 and 80 levels are highly influential, because a great deal of commerce settles around them — that is where the liquidity is. And when bearish in FX, refer to both the 8:30 and the midnight opening price and use the lower one, to set the minimum threshold for a Judas swing to the upside. Bullish reverses everything.
Fluff the exitNever aim for the exact level. Add three to five pips of cushion — ten if you're new. You have a spread to account for and every broker can open it on you. Overzealous targeting has cost him "many amazing exits" over the years.

What news is for: "I don't care about the raw data, I don't care what the expectation is." What he expects is the volatility at those specific times, because the algorithm uses that injection to reprice to levels that let those in the know participate. And the 11:30 rule: if the order hasn't filled by 11:30, pull it — killzones are when you determine the entry, place the order and walk away.

Bias from the daily dealing range (L6)

The bias, stated plainly"It went down to equilibrium or short-term discount. The next day we can expect it to go higher." If it's likely to go higher the next day, you go into that day expecting it — and if that day is down or doesn't move, you carry the same expectation into the next. It isn't hard, but it isn't perfect either.
Dealing range
The most energetic and most recent low and high
Equilibrium
50% of that range — at or below it is a discount
Mean threshold
The midpoint (median) of an order block — half of it

Purge and revert: price runs below a short-term low, purging sell-side liquidity, then reverts back to the high of the last three days — with the purge day counted as day one. Above that high sit the buy stops, because shorts trail their stops there.

Think in pairingsPrice ran below old lows, so smart money bought those sell stops and is now net long. How do they get out? They must sell to willing buyers — the buy stops above the relative equal highs. That's the draw. "If you don't have this mindset going in before you click the enter button of your trade, you are gambling."

Two opening prices, two purposes: midnight NY frames the whole daily range and the London session; 8:30 frames the New York morning; the PM session starts its own accumulation–manipulation–distribution at 1:30. The dip below the 8:30 open is a micro Judas swing — the same Power of Three on a smaller scale. At 8:30, look to the left: find the short-term swing lows, because you want to absorb the sell stops smart money will pair with their buys.

When not to trade (L6)

The line between trading and gamblingIf you cannot reasonably outline where price is going next, higher or lower, you are gambling. And the impulse that you have to be in there every day is a gambler's mentality.
The rattlesnakeA heavily manipulated market is a rattlesnake on the trail. You hear the rattle, you stand still, find it, and back away. When the market is messy, the first thing to consider is closing the charts and going and doing something you love. Going two weeks without a trade will drive some people nuts and make others want to change their style — "and they're both wrong."

Part 4 — Correlation, Tape Reading & Reading the Day

Risk on, risk off (L2)

The mechanismA rising dollar is risk off: a flight to quality, money pouring into the dollar and out of risk assets — so foreign currency, index futures and stocks decline. Dollar down is risk on and they rise. A rising dollar makes sustained FX rallies unlikely and sustained declines likely.

The teeter-totter: dollar and euro are inverted, so if the dollar is dropping into a level you've marked as bullish, expect euro to rally into that same moment — and the instant the dollar reaches its buying opportunity is the instant euro is a short. Use the dollar index for EURUSD and GBPUSD bias.

The day the market refuses (L2)

The read that mattersBearish bias. Did it rally above the midnight opening price? No. Above the 8:30 opening price? No. That combination is the tell: extremely bearish — it can't even rally to a short-term premium at key times, so it will not hand you tidy rallies to fade. Either you get in on small pockets of imbalance, or you miss the move.

Sometimes markets are simply too heavy to rally for you to short into — "it doesn't mean that they've changed anything, it doesn't mean that the concepts don't work." Don't jam the stop: you need time and price behind you before you start moving it. And the mistake he made for years — "it's too low now, the indicators say oversold, I'll buy it" — "1992, 1993, 1994, I blew account after account doing that."

SMT divergence (L3)

Smart money techniqueThe S&P and Nasdaq composites are closely correlated and generally move in tandem — not all the time. If you're bearish and NQ makes the higher run but ES doesn't, the run is a stop run and ES is really weak. That is your confirmation, without an indicator — it looks like a divergence, but it's price.

Plot it with the compare tab, add the correlated contract in a new pane, and change that pane's price source to High when comparing highs (or Low when comparing lows). When a sell program seeks lower prices it is likely to reprice to the low where something was respected — not to a low where a sell-side purge has already occurred.

The Fibonacci settings (L3, L4)

0.5
Equilibrium — the divide between premium and discount
0.62 & 0.79
The optimal trade entry levels
Standard deviations
Projections — how far the move is likely to travel
Which anchor, and whenFor premium and discount, use the range high to low. For OTE he uses the bodies — the lowest open-or-close and the highest open-or-close — because that is the bulk of the volume and the wicks beyond them are stop runs. But on a high-volatility day like FOMC, the extremes of the range should be considered, which means using the wicks. That is the whole answer to why he sometimes uses one and sometimes the other.

Also: any time the market trades above an old high that is a short-term premium, because it's going into liquidity; below an old low is a discount. But things can be in a premium and keep going — market structure only helps you frame an idea, and the idea must align with the present narrative.

News days (L4)

The knee-jerk sequenceFOMC — like non-farm payroll — delivers a repeatable order: an initial leg, then another leg, then a fake-out leg, and then the real setup. That is why you can afford to wait for price to trade into the higher-timeframe gap rather than chasing the first move.
Sweeping a high/low
A shallow run above the level, then back down and a reversal into the range
Running a high/low
It goes right over the top and doesn't look back — an expansion that continues
Eight setups, and you take oneOne move contained eight separate setups — and "I'm not Spiderman", neither are you. Any one could be the setup that fits your unique model. The move that happens ahead of the release doesn't count: "that's a gambler's setup." And none of it is an invitation to trade FOMC or NFP — they are "extremely risky", but a wonderful case study.

The two entry patterns (L5)

1
A level of relative equal highs; price approaches, corrects, approaches again
2
It runs through those highs — buy stops taken
3
Pan back to the left and find the nearest short-term low — that's your trigger
4
Price must trade below that short-term low with displacement
5
Go back through that displacement leg and find the fair value gap — that's where you sell
The filter that removes the low-probability trades"It's not that it goes above the relative equal high and then goes down below that — that's not it, folks." Until it takes out that short-term low with displacement, there's nothing going on. The displacement leg is the foundation of the setup; only then do you look for the gap.

Pattern two is the same logic with an old fair value gap above standing in for the relative equal highs: use the low of the gap as the level, and once price trades up through it go back through the leg for the short-term low, the shift and the displacement. "That way you don't have to worry about how far into it does it need to go."

The day the setup never came (L5)

The model proved itself by keeping him outSell stops were run, but there was no displacement back above the old low and no gap to buy into — so there was no setup and no losing trade. "If a model has valid, reasonable, sound logic behind it, it should prevent you from taking bad trades." It doesn't mean the model's broke — the market did not provide the structure to allow this to form.

Daily rebalance theory (L6)

The routineLook back over the last three days and ask: is there a fair value gap in them? If there isn't, and one day opened, extended and closed near its low, go back to the previous day's low. That level becomes the place a retracement can reach to rebalance the entire move.
What the rebalance does to peopleIt tricks people into thinking the low is in. All it has actually done is go up to a logical level that rebalances the sell-off. Indicator followers see oversold and hunt bullish divergence; breakout traders read it as resistance-turned-support. Don't try to pick the bottom: "I have lost more money trying to do that than any other thing." There's a lot of meat in between the major turning points.
Which liquidity gets taken first — and the hard-line rulePrice running above the buy-side first, without the low below being taken, is bearish and inside the context of the bias. If instead it took the previous low and then rallied, that is not bearish. And: put the fib on the displacement swing — never sell in a discount. "You have to be at equilibrium or higher to be in a premium. That's a hard-line rule — if you just follow that, it will keep you out of so many ill-fated scenarios."

Seasonal tendency: from about the last week of April into May, the S&P, Nasdaq, Dow and Russell tend to be weak — "usually, not always, never guaranteed", like not expecting snow in Maryland on the 4th of July. And the timeline: three months of serious daily study and back testing, then two or three months of forward testing — "after six months with just what's been taught here, I am absolutely confident that you will have found your model."

Part 5 — Session Playbooks & Special Days

Counter-trend, and when it is allowed (L1, L2)

A long against a bearish daily bias is on the table only once the sell side has already been taken and price has already traded into the downside objective. With the downside met, the working idea is a run up into the buy stops — "it doesn't need to do that to be profitable."

Sell side is not enough — it has to be a discountRelative equal lows sitting below are not a buy unless they are in a discount of the day's range. Put the fib on the low and high; the buy goes inside the fair value gap only because it is at or below equilibrium. And the draw does not need to be reached for the framework to pay — think of the level as "a big magnet drawing price up towards it."
The warning attachedCounter-trend trading goes against higher-timeframe order flow. "You can and will absolutely lose money trading this style." It is shown so you understand the retracement and don't trade it — material for back testing, not for live risk.

The PM session playbook (L2, L4)

The lunch-lows ruleMany times when there is an afternoon continuation to the upside, the lunch lows get swept first. If you're bullish and it consolidates through lunch, find the lunch lows, wait for them to be run out, and then the move resumes.

1:30

3:00 pm

What that manipulation does to everyone: breakout buyers get long on the triggered buy stops and are taken against; those without stops are squeezed out; morning longs who trailed their stops below the lunch lows get taken out — "they don't want them being able to profit on the real move"; and the new shorts are about to be squeezed as it rips into the target.

The narrow range day, and close-proximity entries (L3)

The day before the Fed chair speaks is usually a very quiet, small-range day — which is why the market was lethargic. It is important to know when volatility is likely to occur and when it is likely to shrink up. A short that day never qualified: there was a rally, a broken low, a gap and a breakdown — but the displacement leg had no fair value gap above 50% of its own range, so nothing fit.

The rule for a missed entryIf you fumble the ideal entry, getting in really close to it is acceptable — a close-proximity entry. The condition is that you get close to the fair value gap without price running too far away from it. On that move the market gave eight candles of opportunity.

Consolidation days and the three o'clock setup (L6)

The definition and the clockOn a consolidation day the market creates an initial range and stays in it until the afternoon at three o'clock. "It used to be the bond close that started this whole thing, but now it's just three o'clock to four o'clock when equities close" — the closing bell that sets the tone for on-close orders.

The tell that produces one: price trades down to an old low and falls short of it — the setup people read as a double bottom — especially after an outside day with a down close. And when price trades back into the middle of the range the probability statistically moves to 50/50; that's not a disclaimer, it's the read. The afternoon setup, called in advance: run the buy stops above the relative equal highs (squeezing out shorts who trailed, recruiting breakout longs whose new stops build sell side below), then reverse back down into the midpoint of the 9:30 high and low.

Why the chop is expensiveRanges like that can come back on you, stop you out, and then go nowhere for a long period. The advice for anyone getting chewed up in one: wait until three o'clock. On those afternoons nobody is really allowed to make money — and most traders are too afraid to go short into the high, which is precisely where the setup is.

Confluence, not support and resistance (L7)

The gap is the trade, not the levelThe entries occur at old lows where sell side was resting — but the mechanism is a fair value gap after a run below, not broken support turning into resistance. "If the fair value gap doesn't exist, I don't trust that level." The levels aren't indiscriminate: "it's based on liquidity, not support and resistance — the orders I know are going to be there."
Condition 1
Break below the level being watched
Condition 2
A fair value gap formed with that break
Condition 3
The next level breaks too…
Condition 4
…and gets another fair value gap, which delivers even more

Reaching the objective was conditional on all of those coming together — "a confluence, not just one thing." That accumulation is what justifies pushing the button.

The scout and the sniper (L5)

Pointing at a level is not calling a trade: "I'm acting like a scout; you're the sniper in training." The scout shows you where to aim — you scope it and pull the trigger. And don't wait only for the open: if you don't get a trade at 8:30 or 9:30, a fair value gap later in the day still lets you get involved with the move. The trigger that day: three candles forming a swing high inside a five-minute gap, with the gap already traded through and the bias bearish — short on that candle's close.

Negative evidence (L4)

When the market isn't going to deliver, the pattern doesn't appearAn afternoon sell-off was plainly visible on the five-minute — and the model gave no setup in it. It didn't run buy side, it just sold off. "It's important to notice that." The caveat is stated in the same breath: "it's not a fantasy; it means it's not going to work all the time."

Back testing that trains the subconscious (L2)

The methodAnnotate the old move as richly as you can. Then, in the empty space on the chart, write commentary in your own words, in the first person, as though you saw it coming. Screen-capture it into your journal, and re-read those journals at the end of the week.

He states the objection himself — "you're telling people to lie to themselves" — and answers it: "you are lying in the chart when you do your annotations for study purposes. You're tricking your brain." Your subconscious retains the image and the words, so when you see it live it remembers having done it before: pseudo-experience. The comparison is the reticular activating system — buy a car and suddenly everyone has bought the same one, because it has become meaningful to you.

And what never goes in itNever write anything negative — not "I wish I'd seen this", not "I was foolish", not "I'm never going to get this." These entries exist to be reflected on later, so they must be positive. Also: "you're going to develop at your own pace and arrive at full understanding right on time."

Part 6 — Dealing Range, Algorithmic Theory & Risk

The Sunday opening gap, and drawing the dealing range (L1)

The Sunday gapPut the Sunday gap opening on your chart and plot it across the entire week. There is often a lot of valuation around it, so treat it as a dynamic level: above it, look for support; below it, look for resistance. It earns its keep in consolidation weeks — an FOMC week, for instance — not in weeks that run away and never come back.
The correction on the dealing rangeAnchor the fib on the expansion swing — the impulsive leg running in the direction you are trying to trade. Looking for a long? Draw from the low up to the high of that up-leg, then wait for the retracement; below 50% is the discount where you hunt the long, better still if it lands in a gap. Don't use a high-to-low leg to measure something you're trying to go long on. The measurement always follows the direction of the trade you want.

The mean threshold (L2)

The three sensitive pricesWhen a candle is used as an order block, the low, the open and the middle of the range are the three sensitive areas. The middle — 50% of the candle, found with a fib across it — is the mean threshold, and it is the level the block has to defend. If it doesn't hold, expect the order block to fail.

Where a delivery came in two stages, measure the most recent one: short-term low to short-term high, fib across, 50% is equilibrium, and expect the drop into discount. The same shape repeats fractally up the hourly — dealing range, retrace into discount, gap or block, rally, then a new dealing range and the same again.

SMT — timing, and the trap (L2)

The cracking correlation shows up at specific times — 8:30, 9:30, and at news releases. A nice run early, before or around 7:00 am, means a deep retracement is probably coming, and SMT usually shows up around 9:30 after such a run.

The trap"If you have a bias it's helpful, but if you don't know where it's going you're going to encounter what would look like SMT divergence and then it disappears" as the two start moving in concert again. Hence the standing instruction: know what price is reaching for. SMT without a bias is worthless.

No entry means no setup (L3)

Negative evidence is evidenceOn a big drop, "notice there's no model entry here. That's telling — it's tipping its hand to you, because there's no real setup and it's a rush to get down here." A run that never offers an entry is not a delivery you were meant to be in; it is the market clearing the liquidity before it does what it actually intends.

And which market structure shift counts? It is not a rule about the candles — it's the frame: define the range you are working within first, and the shift is the one that breaks that range.

Where bias comes from, and the days you skip (L4)

Off the daily: price moves off a low and creates a swing low, so it's easy to assume it wants to come back to the high — bullish until that high is taken out. After the high, a day or two of retracement is logical because the rally left a gap; price trades down, fills it, tests it, and rallies. Levels on the hourly are transposed daily levels, not hourly support and resistance — lose that context on a one-minute chart and you won't understand why price is dropping where it is.

Non-Farm Payroll: study it, don't trade itNFP Friday is not a day to speculate on. Not because money can't be made there — because you are here to learn to read price action, the day can be very volatile, choppy, or a complete dud, and precision drops precipitously. Determine which side of liquidity price is reaching for before the 8:30 release, then watch the one- and five-minute charts: "an amazing study for liquidity purposes only."
Stop trading by WednesdayStop by the New York session on Wednesday. If you were profitable to that point, stop for the rest of the week and just observe. And if you have bagged nothing from Sunday's open through Wednesday, don't do any trades — sit with the desire and do nothing. It forges discipline and patience, and Thursday/Friday of an NFP week lose a lot of their precision.

Changing gears — abandoning a bias (L5)

The evidence the bias was wrong: multiple lows were taken and the market reversed while offering no high-probability short; the downside objective was never tagged and that liquidity was left in place. The turn came on the bellwether 15-minute — price ran higher, gave a short-term shift in market structure, created a gap, and traded back down into it. "No panic. No 'I'm missing a move'. No calling somebody or reaching out on social media."

Narrative, defined"Narrative is the understanding of what price should do, why, and what things it will encounter to prove that the narrative you are assuming is in fact underway." It is not buying and selling pressure — it is algorithmic. But narrative by itself is not an entry: you still want displacement, then measure that leg for premium and discount, and wait for the discount.

Retraces into lunch

That distinction was learned from a floor trader in the open-outcry days: when the floor knew the market was in a hurry, they didn't leave the floor — and the same mentality transferred into electronic trading.

The five-point model (L6)

Five points, then outFive points, then out — even if the framework allows for thirty. Below five you are ultra short-term scalping and commissions will kill you; five is the lowest threshold a setup should reasonably yield.
One trade in the morning
8:30 or 9:30 — get the five points at 8:30 and he doesn't trade 9:30. Miss or lose at 8:30, try 9:30.
One in the afternoon
Only if the morning was missed or lost. If the morning made money, the afternoon is demo.
Why not push the edge
The lesson is discipline, not greed. Seeing that you can make more and choosing not to builds the contentment that stops over-trading.
The arithmetic
One ES contract, one point is $50 → five points is $250 a day; consistently, that's 25 points — $1,250 — a week.

The six keys to daily bias (L7)

The rules, in order
  1. A bias every day is unrealistic. Going in with a predetermined daily bias before the market opens, invariably it will be wrong.
  2. Determine the likely weekly expansion from the weekly chart — not where the week will close, where it is likely to reach for. That gives the strongest bias.
  3. Look for obvious liquidity in that direction — below old lows, above old highs.
  4. Identify imbalances top down — weekly, daily, 4h, 1h, 15m, then 5 down through 1 for whichever gives the clear gap under the model's rules.
  5. Focus only on high or medium impact calendar days. Yellow events are of no interest.
  6. Look for the directional price run inside the killzones, at the same time the calendar says a high or medium impact event is likely.
The framing question that goes with it: who's in the crosshairs? Have people been making money going long? Is there a low to run down and stop them out with?
The three-bar swing
A sweep of an old high followed by that three-bar pattern is when lower prices are expected — confirmed when the next candle trades lower. Not a five-candle fractal: "if you're waiting for five candles you missed the boat."
10:00–11:00 am
The London close profit-taking hour. Usually the opposing end of the daily range forms here — if the session high is in, the low likely forms in this window. Have roughly 80% of a directional trade off between 10 and 11.
Wick days
Two big wicks either side caused by news? Ignore the wicks — the manipulation is done and can't hurt you. Take the real range from the swing that follows.
Protected lows
London gives an energetic move, price retraces, and at 8:30 you open near the London low → you can buy before 9:30, because the manipulation already happened. Stops go under those lows.
Why not trade every day"Trading is not an Olympic sport. They don't give out gold medals for over-trading — but they do blow accounts."

Risk, drawdown and stop management (L8)

The gold standard setup
  1. Price trades down into and closes in the 15-minute fair value gap.
  2. A short-term shift in market structure — it shows willingness to go higher.
  3. It trades back down into the order block — the last down-close candle — with a fair value gap there.
  4. That is also an optimal trade entry, the 62–70% retracement, below equilibrium.
Fair value gap + order block + OTE, after a short-term shift in market structure is the flagship pattern of the channel — the gold standard of the ICT setup.
The drawdown ladderTake a full 1% loss → the next trade risks 0.5%. Lose that → the next risks 0.25%, which is the floor. You must make back 50% of what you lost before returning to full risk. Three consecutive losses at 1%, 0.5% and 0.25% leaves you down 1¾% — easy to come back from. The alternative — doubling up to 2%, then 3% or 4%, widening or dropping the stop — reaches 10% drawdown quickly, "along with embarrassment and shame."
When the stop is allowed to moveAt 50% of the expected targeted range, the stop can be trimmed by 25% of the entry-to-stop distance. At 75% of the expected range, the stop goes to break even — period.
The hard stop is not optional, and the tax"I don't really use a hard stop but I know when I want to get out" — if you don't know where to place a stop loss, you really don't know what you're doing. And the closing word: you are going to have losing trades, plural. That is a tax on success. Nobody gets around it and nobody is the exception. What you control is how bad it gets. Professional money management views drawdown as a loan you are going to collect interest on — a bank doesn't cry about lending you the money and doesn't want it back straight away.

The checklist before any trade

  1. What is the draw? Is price reaching for an imbalance, or for a liquidity pool? Name the level before anything else.
  2. What is the weekly and daily bias? Where is the week likely to reach for; where does the daily dealing range sit relative to equilibrium?
  3. Is it the right time? 8:30, 9:30, 1:30, or 3:00–4:00 — inside a killzone, with a red or orange calendar event if one is due.
  4. Has liquidity been taken? No stop run, no market structure shift — and a shift without one is not a shift.
  5. Is there displacement? Energetic and animated, preferably closing beyond the level. A lethargic drift is not enough.
  6. Is there a fair value gap inside that displacement leg? No gap, no trade. Strip down 5 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 and take the first one you find.
  7. Premium or discount? Sells at or above equilibrium; buys at or below it. Never sell in a discount — that is a hard-line rule.
  8. Where is the stop and where is the target? Stop beyond the candle that created the gap (or the swing extreme); target the external liquidity, fluffed a few pips inside.
  9. Is the risk right? 1% maximum, less while learning; biggest position first if you pyramid; and don't move the stop until time and price are behind you.

What the mentorship keeps warning you about

The repeated warnings
  • Don't chase. Once the low is taken you are chasing; learn to sell while the candle is going up.
  • Don't trade every day. "Trading is not an Olympic sport." One setup a week is enough, and stop by Wednesday if you've banked it.
  • Don't trade the noon hour, or NFP and FOMC. They are case studies, not trading days.
  • Don't pick bottoms or tops. "I have lost more money trying to do that than any other thing."
  • Don't call it a setup when there isn't one. No entry means no setup — that is the market tipping its hand, and the model keeping you out.
  • Don't force it when structure disagrees. A short-term high trading above a rebalance ITH means your idea is probably flawed — go to the sidelines.
  • Don't strangle the position. Trailing too tight and rushing out of marginal winners are the two failure modes that filter people out of this industry.
  • Don't increase risk after a loss. Halve it, then quarter it, and make back half of what you lost first.
  • Don't dilute the model with bolted-on indicators, and don't grow a dependency on being shown every move — "that mindset stunts your growth."
  • Don't skip the journal. "Nothing else gives it to you. Watching my videos will not do it for you. If you fail under my tutelage, it's because you don't do this."
